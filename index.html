What the score is

Five creeks, one pan.

Google, Yelp, TripAdvisor, OpenTable and DoorDash are five different streams, each with its own gravel. Pulse works all of them, washes out the sand (a strict crowd on one, a generous one on another, three reviews pretending to be thirty) and hands you what settles in the pan: one 0–100 number that means the same thing for a food truck in Homer and a steakhouse in Anchorage.

Every creek, worked

Google, Yelp, TripAdvisor, OpenTable and DoorDash today, with more as we add them. We cite each source by name. We never republish their ratings; ours is the complete picture, theirs are parts of it.

The sand washed out

Each platform is measured against its own statewide baseline before anything is combined, so a strict Yelp crowd doesn't drag you down and a generous delivery app doesn't prop you up.

Weighed, not counted

Two hundred reviews outweigh six. A new listing with three five-stars isn't a 100; it's a promising start, and the score says so until the reviews back it up.

Dine-in and take-out, separately

The kitchen that wins the dining room can lose the doorstep.

Nobody else splits it. Pulse scores the people who sat down (Google, Yelp, TripAdvisor, OpenTable) apart from the people who ordered in (DoorDash and the delivery apps). A wide gap between the two is the most useful thing on the page: it tells you whether the problem is the food, the packaging, or the forty minutes in a driver's back seat.

Take-out scores appear once a business has enough delivery-platform history; 272 Alaska businesses have one today, and the number grows every sweep.

What a higher score is worth

Reviews compound. The score tells you which direction.

Since we began tracking in July, Alaska businesses scoring 90 or better have added Google reviews at roughly 3 times the rate of businesses under 40. Happy customers are asked and say yes; unhappy ones show up on their own. The score is where that curve puts you today, and the bands are how far you are from the next one.

The leaders ask at the moment of payment.

While the visit is still warm and the phone is already out. Businesses that ask add reviews several times faster than those that wait, and the reviews that arrive unasked skew unhappy. That single habit is most of the gap between the top band and the middle.

Every claimed business sees its own band, its next threshold, and where it stands among its city and its category. How the score is built.

Private by design

Your score is yours. Nothing about you is public.

Pulse exists to help Alaska businesses build, not to rank them in public. A business's full picture is shown only to the people who run it, after they claim it. Signed-in owners also see who leads their town and category, and nothing else about anyone.

  • No public scores. The lookup confirms we track a business and nothing more. No number, no grade, no rank appears anywhere you don't have to sign in. Behind sign-in, we name the top five in your town and category, upward only; no business is ever shown at the bottom of a list.
  • Only the owner sees it. Claiming a business takes a verified email, with a phone number on file. Competitors can't get in.
  • We only publish upward. When Pulse recognizes businesses publicly, it names the ones at the top. There is no bottom list, and there never will be.
  • We cite, we don't copy. Sources are named; their individual ratings and review text stay on their own sites.
